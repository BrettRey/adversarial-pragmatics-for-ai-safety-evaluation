**Why EA cares.** This is a record-adequacy investigation conducted in public, over 141,006 transcripts, with an outcome on each of EA's four verdict families. It is closer to EA's object than most of the literature the paper currently cites, because the question actually asked was what the surviving record established about actions already taken.

**Record adequacy (\(J_R\)).** The record was sufficient to reconstruct what happened, and the post says so implicitly by reconstructing it. But it also concedes the record was not adequate for timely review: "Both we and our partner also could have reviewed evaluation transcripts or network logs more thoroughly," and the misconfiguration went undetected until monitoring caught it months after the earliest April incidents. Adequate for retrospective forensics, inadequate for the review that would have mattered. That is the distinction EA draws between an unevaluable record and an adequate record whose showing falls short, made by a real case.

**A record type EA doesn't currently treat.** The transcripts contain the models' own verbalised reasoning about whether the environment was real, including the Incident 2 model concluding that publishing to the real internet would be "NOT okay, and surely not the intended solution" and then reasoning back to simulation on unrecognised certificate authorities and a 2026 system date. EA's evidence primitives cover authority, configuration, context, tool use, oversight, and preservation. A first-person reasoning trace is none of those, and it is doing real evidentiary work here. Worth deciding whether it is a distinct evidence domain or whether EA should say why it isn't.

**Answerability (\(J_B\)), and this is the sharpest fit.** The post says a "misunderstanding between us and our evaluation partner" caused the misconfiguration, then assigns the bearer by declaration: "consistent with a blameless postmortem culture, we're approaching the fixes as if the responsibility were ours alone." So duty here is voluntarily assumed rather than established, and the underlying situation is exactly EA's piecemeal problem: identity, duty, and reachability distributed across Anthropic, Irregular, and the three affected organizations, with no single party holding all of them by any independent route. A declared bearer is not the same as an identified one, and EA has the vocabulary to say why.

**Forum capacity (\(J_C\)).** The strongest single fact for EA in the whole post: two of the three affected organizations "had not previously detected the activity" and learned of it only when Anthropic contacted them. The parties with standing to complain had no route to knowing they had been compromised. A forum existed in principle for each of them and had no access, which is precisely what \(J_C\) is built to report and what an aggregate assurance score would conceal.

**Where it could go.** Section 3's governance material, alongside Robodebt. Robodebt shows a complete record that could not establish authorization; this shows a record adequate for reconstruction whose subjects could not reach it. Two different failures of the same relation, one public-sector and adjudicated, one private and self-reported.

**Caution.** First-party disclosure by the vendor whose models are the subject, so the bearer assignment above is that party's own account of where responsibility sits, which is a claim EA's apparatus should test rather than adopt. METR review and a redacted transcript were promised; both would change what this case can carry.
